IS-100 · Introduction to the Incident Command System (ICS)

19

Who does what

Incident Commander (IC)The boss of the whole emergency. There is always one.

Command Staff · the IC’s helpers

Public Information Officer (PIO)Talks to the news and the public
Safety OfficerCan stop anything unsafe, right away
Liaison OfficerTalks to the other agencies
OperationsThe doers: do the hands-on work
PlanningThe planners: write the Incident Action Plan (IAP)
LogisticsThe getters: food, radios, vehicles, supplies
Finance / AdminThe payers: track costs and time

General Staff · four section chiefs

One boss, three helpers, four sections: doers, planners, getters, payers.

  • Like a school play: the director is the incident commander (IC); the actors are Operations; the script writer is Planning; the props crew is Logistics; the ticket booth is Finance.
  • Only fill the boxes you need. A small emergency may be the IC alone, doing every job.
  • Ask: who talks to the news? (Public Information Officer.) Who can stop unsafe work? (Safety Officer.)
  • Unified Command: when several agencies share the lead, they agree on one plan and each keeps its own authority.

Four rules that keep it orderly

Span of control
123456789
Too few3 to 7 people · 5 is bestToo many: add a leader
Unity of command
One bossYou

Orders come only from your boss. Anyone can share information.

Common terminology

Say it in plain English. No codes or slang.

Dispatch
1Asked for
2Sent
3Check in

Never just show up. Wait to be asked.

Small teams, one boss each, plain words, and wait to be asked.

  • Span of control: like a teacher with a small group. 3 to 7 people per leader; 5 is best.
  • Unity of command: one boss each, so you never get two different orders.
  • Ask: what is wrong with “10-8, back to the barn”? (Codes and slang; not everyone knows them.)
  • Showing up without being asked (self-dispatching) clogs the scene and nobody knows you are there.
  • Accountability: check in, follow the plan, and leaders keep track of everyone.

On sceneIncident CommandDoes the work right now.Players on the field

  • Emergency Operations Centers (EOCs) are off site. They support the scene; they do not run it.
  • The MAC Group only steps in when there isn’t enough to go around.
  • Public Information Officers work together in the Joint Information Center so the public gets one message.
  • Ask: two news crews hear different stories. Which part failed? (Joint Information System.)

Part 1 · Stress care: sooner is better

BeforeLearn what stress does, so it hits less hard
Incident
Within hoursDefusing: a short talk

AfterFriends, support and more help if needed
Sooner is better

CISD means Critical Incident Stress Debriefing: a group talk with trained helpers, 1 to 3 days after a hard event.

Talk about it early. Help works best soon after.

  • This is Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM): a set of tools, not one meeting.
  • It is like first aid for feelings, not therapy.
  • Ask: when does the group debriefing happen? (24 to 72 hours after.)

IS-200 · Basic ICS for Initial Response

31

Incident types: Type 1 is the biggest

Type 5Car crashA few responders. Over in hours.No written plan
Type 4House fireSeveral crews. One shift.No written plan
Type 3Large wildfireSection chiefs added. Lasts days.Written plan
Type 2Regional floodHelp from the region. Up to 200 people a shift.Written plan
Type 1Major hurricaneNational help. 500+ people a shift.Written plan

Examples are typical, not fixed. Size and complexity set the type.

Lower number, bigger incident. A written plan starts at Type 3.

  • Backwards on purpose: Type 5 is smallest, Type 1 is largest.
  • The written plan is the Incident Action Plan (IAP).
  • Ask: a tornado wipes out a whole county for weeks. What type? (Type 1 or 2.)
  • Deciding the type by predicting what you will need is complexity analysis.

Commander, Chief, Director, Supervisor, Leader.

  • Memory trick: Can Chiefs Direct Super Leaders? Commander, Chief, Director, Supervisor, Leader.
  • Deputies only in the top three. A deputy is a backup boss who can take over, and must be fully qualified.
  • Ask: is “north side of the fire” a Division or a Group? (Division: a place.) “Medical”? (Group: a job.)
  • Strike Team: five ambulances. Task Force: an ambulance, an engine and a rescue truck.

IS-200 · Basic ICS for Initial Response

33

Changing who is in charge

1ArriveThe new incident commander (IC) looks over the scene.
2Brief face to faceThe old IC explains everything, using ICS Form 201.
3Pick the timeBoth agree on the exact handoff time.
4Tell everyoneEveryone hears who is in charge, and from when.
Old IC in chargeHandoff timeNew IC in charge

Like passing a relay baton: face to face, at a set time, and everyone is told.

  • Until the handoff time, the old incident commander is still in charge. Never zero bosses, never two.
  • A more qualified person who arrives may take over, leave it as it is, or ask for someone even more qualified.
  • Ask: why not just hand off by text? (Things get missed; the briefing must be face to face.)

Big plan, then goals, then today’s plan, then briefings, then your job.

  • Every job you get traces back to the goals at the top. That is management by objectives.
  • The incident commander or Unified Command sets the goals. Planning writes the plan; the IC approves it.
  • Ask: who runs the operational period briefing? (Planning Section Chief.)

A good goal is specific

Writing an objective

Too broad“Find the missing hiker.”No place, no time, no way to know when it’s done.
Specific and measurable“Search segments 3 and 4 by 1800 hours and report to the Division Supervisor.”Where, by when, and what done looks like.

Say where, by when, and what “done” looks like.

  • Game: turn “Clean your room” into a specific, measurable goal. (“Clothes in the hamper and bed made by 7 pm.”)
  • Test trap: big, inspiring goals are wrong answers. The test wants specific and measurable.

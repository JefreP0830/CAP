FEMA Student ID (SID)

Register once. Use the same SID for IS-100, IS-200, IS-700 and IS-800.

Register at cdp.dhs.gov/femasid. The FEMA courses are not on that site; they are at training.fema.gov/is.

Have ready

  • Your full legal name (no nicknames)
  • An email only you use. One email can hold one SID, so siblings cannot share a parent’s address
  • A phone number
  • Date, city and state of birth
  • Three short security answers (one word is best)
  • A password: 14+ characters, with a number, an upper and lower case letter, and one of $ % ^ , ! # - _ + = ; : ~
  • Age 10 or older to receive FEMA exam credit

Step 1

Register

  1. Already have one? Use Forgot your FEMA SID? first. It emails it to you. Do not make a second one.
  2. Go to Register for a FEMA SID.
  3. Fill in name, email, phone, birth details and the three security questions. An alternate email is optional; password resets go there too.
  4. Create the password.
  5. Check I agree to the Privacy Statement, then select Register.
  6. Write your SID number down. It is also emailed to you.

Step 2

First login

  1. At cdp.dhs.gov/femasid select Log in to FEMA SID. Enter your SID number and password.
  2. Pick a second factor: Authenticator App (recommended), Mobile Phone (text or call), or Email. Then Continue.
  3. Enter the 6-digit code and select Verify.
  4. That device is remembered for 21 days unless you clear its cookies.
  5. Check your profile details. Fix anything wrong now.

Step 3

Take the exam

  1. Open the course page from the guide for that test, or search the code (for example IS-100.c) at training.fema.gov/is.
  2. Select Take Final Exam. Enter your SID when asked.
  3. Answer every question. 75% passes.
  4. Your certificate appears in the NDEMU Student Portal right away and is emailed to you. Save the PDF; you will need it as proof of completion.

Stuck?

SID, password or MFA problemsFEMA SID Help Desk, 866-291-0696, weekdays 7:30 to 4:30 Central. Contact form on cdp.dhs.gov/femasid.
Exam or certificate problemsFEMA Independent Study Program, 301-447-1200, weekdays 8:00 to 6:00 Eastern.

CAPT 116 · General Emergency Services

CAPR 60-3(I), Emergency Services Training and Operational Missions (26 Dec 12)

Take it in eServices. It is the starting point for every emergency services qualification.

Core essentials

Support prioritywhen resources are short (§1-4)
1Air ForceHighest
2Other DoD
3Federal civilFEMA, FAA
4State
5LocalLowest
Minimum team makeupbefore release
Aircrew of 3Mission Pilot flies. Mission Observer helps with navigation, radios and the search. Mission Scanner watches outside for the search objective.
Ground team of 4+Qualified Ground Team Leader plus at least 3 qualified members or supervised trainees. Works at the level of its lowest qualified member.
On a missionrules in the field
  • Safety must always be a primary concern (§1-1b).
  • CAPR 60-3 gives direction for mission training, qualification and actual missions. Where it is silent, use common sense and prudent judgment.
  • C911 missions are imminently serious, where life may be at stake. Only the wing commander or the commander’s designee may authorize one (§1-9a).
  • Air Force-assigned missions: Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) liability protection for members; FECA injury benefits for members 18 and older, so a cadet under 18 is not covered by FECA (§1-24a, b).
  • Private property: entry may be justified to save life, but make every effort to get the controlling agency’s approval and the owner’s consent (§1-24c).
  • Night vision devices: on foot or as a passenger, never by a driver (§1-30a). Patient transport: only in an emergency when other suitable transport is not readily available (§1-22).
  • Never alter, separate or misuse communications or advanced technology equipment (§1-36a).
Qualifications and recordspaperwork that keeps you mission-ready
  • CAPF 101 shows what you may do, as trainee or qualified. Ratings stay valid through the last day of the 36th month (§2-2d).
  • Every emergency services member completes OPSEC training and signs the nondisclosure agreement (§1-35).
  • Alert rosters: update at least annually, and reissue after major changes (§1-5c(6)).
  • Each wing sends its annual operations training plan to the liaison region in WMIRS by 31 July for the next fiscal year (§1-7).
  • The IC makes sure WMIRS entries and approvals are complete before any sortie (§1-15a). Electronic records are fine if backed up (§1-5b(3)(b)).
  • Supplements to CAPR 60-3: wing level or higher only (Congressional Squadron excepted), with region commander, national operations and Air Force liaison approval (§1-2).

CAPT 117 · ES Continuing Education

Part 1 Critical Incident Stress (CAPR 60-5) · Part 2 Crew Resource Management · Part 3 Mission Base

Take it in eServices. Three parts.

Core essentials

CISM timelinePart 1, sooner beats later
1BeforePre-incident education. Reduces the harmful effects of trauma.
2Within hoursDefusing
324 to 72 hoursDebriefing (CISD)
4AfterPeer support and referral
  • CAPR 60-5 (3 Nov 06) covers critical incident stress management.
  • CISM is many tools, not one procedure. Sooner is better.
  • Crisis intervention is short-term emotional first aid, a “psychological Band-Aid”, not therapy.
Stress reactionsPart 1, four places stress shows up
CognitiveConfusion, poor memory
EmotionalAnxiety, grief, guilt
PhysicalFatigue, rapid pulse
BehavioralWithdrawal, pacing
  • Critical incident stress is not new: shell shock (World War I), combat fatigue, PTSD.
  • Long-term stress accelerates aging and harms health.
Crew resource managementPart 2, the whole crew flies the mission
  • CRM is every crew member’s job, not just the pilot in command’s. It is about teamwork, communication, attitudes and behavior, not stick skills.
  • Accidents come from a chain of factors, not one bad decision.
  • Split the workload to limit fatigue and keep the crew effective longer. Announce what you are doing. Acknowledge, do it, report it done.
  • Prioritize: aviate (fly the aircraft first), navigate, communicate. Sterile cockpit applies to everyone aboard.
  • Decision makers need correct, relevant information they can control, not everything.
  • Standard terms and signals exist for aircrew and flightline so everyone hears the same message.
Mission basePart 3, arriving, working and leaving
  • Before you leave: know where and when to report, and to whom. Check in at any designated location (command post, base, camp, staging area, helibase).
  • Sent straight to a tactical assignment? Report to the division or group supervisor, or the operations chief or IC.
  • Unit log (ICS 214): kept by supervisors, updated every operational period. Use 24-hour time. Radio: clear text, short messages.
  • Pack for an extended stay; supplies at the incident may be limited. Finish your assignments, return gear as directed and check out before you leave.

FEMA Independent Study

IS-100 · Introduction to the Incident Command System

ICS-100 · who does what, and the rules that keep it orderly

Take it at training.fema.gov/is (IS-100.c). You need a FEMA SID to submit the exam. 75% passes.

Core essentials

The ICS organizationwho does what
Incident CommanderAlways staffed. Does every job not yet assigned.

Command Staff · advise the IC

Public Information OfficerOnly voice to the media
Safety OfficerCan stop unsafe work at once
Liaison OfficerContact for other agencies
OperationsDoes the tactical work
PlanningTracks resources, information, documents. Writes the IAP.
LogisticsFood, comms, medical, transport, facilities
Finance / AdminCosts, time, claims, mutual aid paperwork

General Staff · section chiefs, who run the work

  • The IC sets objectives (or Unified Command), and establishes and expands the organization only as needed (modular).
  • Unified Command: agencies set shared incident objectives and keep their own authority.
  • Transfer of command always includes a briefing.
  • Incident Command sets up support facilities as the incident needs them.
Rules that keep it orderlyNIMS management characteristics
  • ICS works for any incident or planned event, of any size. It sits in the NIMS Command and Coordination component.
  • Span of control: one supervisor manages 3 to 7 people; 5 is best. Too many? Add a supervisor and reorganize.
  • Unity of command: each person reports to one supervisor.
  • Common terminology: standard names for positions, facilities and resources. Plain language on the radio; no 10-codes or agency jargon.
  • Dispatch/deployment: resources go only when requested through established systems. No self-dispatching.
  • Integrated communications supports shared situational awareness. Information and intelligence management covers gathering data, such as weather from technical specialists.
  • Accountability = check-in, incident action planning, personal responsibility, resource tracking.
  • Coordination is not command: directing, ordering or controlling is command.

NRF · always in effect, a guide for how the nation responds

Take it at training.fema.gov/is (IS-800.d). You need a FEMA SID to submit the exam. 75% passes.

Core essentials

Tiered responsebegin and end locally
1Local and tribalFirst to arrive, last to leave. Police, fire, EMS. IC relays needs to the local EOC.
2StateGovernor and state EOC. Other states help through EMAC (interstate mutual aid).
3FederalStafford Act: FEMA leads; the Unified Coordination Group leads at the Joint Field Office (JFO). Non-Stafford: agency with primary legal authority.
  • Tiered response: incidents are handled at the lowest jurisdictional level possible.
  • Tribal governments may deal directly with the federal government.
  • NOC: DHS principal operations center. RRCC: FEMA regional center. Many EOCs use the ICS structure.
  • MAC Group: senior officials who can commit agency resources and funds.
  • Unified Command benefit: joint priorities.
Five mission areaswhat the nation does
PreventionStop imminent terrorism
ProtectionSecure people and assets
MitigationReduce loss of life and property by lessening disaster impact
ResponseSave lives, protect property and environment, meet basic needs
RecoveryRestore and rebuild
  • Three core capabilities cross all mission areas and lifelines: Planning; Public Information and Warning; Operational Coordination.
  • The National Preparedness Goal sets the vision and names the core capabilities. Operational Communications means timely communications among responders and affected communities.
Seven community lifelinesthe basics a community runs on
Safety and Security
Food, Water, Shelter
Health and Medical
Energy
Communications
Transportation
Hazardous Materials
  • Community lifelines: services that keep critical government and business functions running.
  • The course teaches seven lifelines. FEMA added an eighth, Water Systems, in 2023.
The NRF itselfhow the framework works
  • The NRF is always in effect and scales to the incident. It covers the Response mission area and how it ties to the others, and aligns with NIMS.
  • NRF guiding principles include engaged partnership (whole community, information sharing) and scalable, flexible, adaptable capabilities.
  • Engaging communities shares information and builds readiness.
  • ESFs: government and private-sector groups, not only federal. ESF #6 works with the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) to identify children and reunite them with families.
